About this companion

An independent companion book

This is a standalone companion to the Body Recomp Bible editorial world. It is not one of the five canonical Body Recomp Bible volumes. It borrows the calm, practical, non-shaming voice and the minimal visual system of The Recomp Kitchen — white pages, charcoal type, one clear blue accent — then adds a playful register for young cooks.

The kitchen rule

Dessert does not need to dress up as medicine. It can offer flavor, connection, and kitchen practice. No food is a reward, and no child has to earn something sweet.

Who it is for

Families with children about 5–12 and their adult caregivers. Adults stay responsible for heat, knives, appliances, allergens, food safety, and age-appropriate textures.

Who needs extra guidance

Children under 5, and any child with swallowing, developmental, allergy, or medical needs, require individualized adult and professional guidance. This book does not replace it.

Development status — please read

Every recipe here is a development draft. Recipes have not been kitchen-tested, and this book publishes no calories, macros, verified yields, or shelf-life. Before anything here could be called final it must pass: kitchen testing · sensory testing · standardized ingredient weights · verified yield · allergen review · food-safety review · storage validation · pediatric or qualified nutrition review · and Cristy Sim’s final approval.

Nothing in this book is described as final, tested, approved, or publication-ready.

Welcome to the Family Dessert Kitchen

This book starts with a simple idea: dessert can be a pleasant, predictable part of family life. There is no need to hide ingredients, turn every recipe into a nutrition promise, or ask a child to earn something sweet. The recipes use familiar ingredients and small, real tasks so children can genuinely take part — while an adult stays in charge of the parts that need care.

For the young cook

  • You are a real cook here, not a helper.
  • You get to notice, measure, mix, taste, and invent.
  • You never have to finish a food or love every flavor.
  • Saying “I am done” or “not yet” is allowed.

For the adult

  • You handle heat, knives, appliances, and allergens.
  • You choose safe sizes and textures for your child.
  • You keep the mood calm — no bargaining over bites.
  • You decide what fits your family and your kitchen.

How this book teaches

Every recipe carries a small learning activity — a thing to notice, compare, count, or smell. The goal is curiosity and skill, never pressure to eat. Alongside the recipes you will find a Flavor Lab, a Nutrition Explorer, kitchen games, and a testing journal, all built around the same idea: cooking is a wonderful way to learn.

A note on words we do not use

You will not see foods called clean, dirty, good, bad, guilt-free, or sinful. Food gives the body energy and building blocks; it is not a moral test. We describe flavor and texture instead — creamy, tart, warm, crunchy — because those are the words that help a young cook grow.

Adult Safety Guide

Children can do a great deal in the kitchen when the risky parts are clearly an adult's job. This guide is written for you, the caregiver. Read it once before you begin, and keep the calm, matter-of-fact tone the recipes use — safety works best without fear.

Supervision & hygiene

  • Stay within arm’s reach of young cooks the whole time.
  • Everyone washes hands with soap and warm water before cooking, after handling raw egg, and after using the bathroom or touching pets.
  • Tie back hair, roll up sleeves, and wipe surfaces before you start.
  • Keep raw egg away from foods that will not be cooked.

Heat & burn prevention

  • The adult manages the oven, stove, and anything hot.
  • Turn pot handles inward; use dry oven mitts.
  • Let baked and warm desserts cool from hot to warm before a child tastes them.
  • Baked fruit and its juices hold heat — cool well and check before serving.

Knives, blenders & appliances

  • The adult does all cutting and runs blenders and food processors.
  • Unplug appliances before scraping the jar; keep fingers away from blades.
  • Give children safe tools instead: spoons, whisks, cups, mashers, and clean hands.

Perishables & doneness

  • Keep yogurt, milk, and egg mixtures cold until use; do not leave them out on the counter.
  • Cook every egg-containing recipe fully — no wet or runny centers.
  • Serve freshly; this draft does not provide validated storage times.

If you remember only one thing

You decide what is safe for your child today — the right size, the right texture, the right amount of help. When in doubt, make the piece smaller, the task simpler, and the supervision closer.

Choking, texture & age

Match every piece and consistency to your child's skills. The foods below are common choking hazards for young children and need adult judgment — cut small, modify the texture, or leave out.

Cut, modify, or skip for the young

  • Whole or round fruit — quarter grapes and berries lengthwise.
  • Whole nuts and hard, dense pieces.
  • Thick, sticky spoonfuls of nut or seed butter — spread thin.
  • Large chunks of dried fruit such as raisins — chop or omit for the very young.
  • Hard, still-frozen pieces — let soften first.

Build safer textures

  • Blend, mash, or finely chop to suit the child.
  • Thin nut or seed butter with yogurt or milk.
  • Serve pops and bark once slightly softened.
  • Offer water alongside; children eat seated and calm, never while walking or playing.

Allergens: name them, do not guess

Read every label, every time. A substitution changes flavor, structure, and the allergen profile. Recipes list allergens to check, but brands vary — the label is the authority. Common allergens to watch in this book include milk, egg, wheat (via oats with cross-contact), tree nuts, peanuts, soy, and seeds.

If your child has a diagnosed allergy, follow the plan from your family’s healthcare professional. This book cannot replace that plan.

Oats & cross-contact

Oats are naturally wheat-free but are often processed near wheat. If wheat is a concern, choose oats labeled to address cross-contact and confirm on the package.

Meet the Dessert Detectives

A Dessert Detective is a young cook who notices things. Detectives do not judge food as good or bad — they investigate flavor, texture, aroma, color, and safety. Throughout this book you will find numbered missions. Some are quick questions; some ask you to draw, measure, or invent.

There are no rewards for eating and no punishments for leaving food. The reward is learning something together.

Detective Badge

The Detective Code

  • I wash my hands first.
  • I notice before I decide.
  • I use kind, curious words.

My Detective Powers

  • Looking closely
  • Smelling carefully
  • Measuring exactly
  • Describing clearly

My Superpower

Saying “I am done,” “I want to try,” or “not that texture yet” is real detective skill, too.

Recipe 01 · Frozen & Fruity
Creamy Mango Yogurt Pops
DEVELOPMENT RECIPE — REQUIRES KITCHEN TESTING
 Estimated time 10 min + freezing   ·   Estimated yield about 8 small pops (estimated)
Do not publish or assign nutrition values before testing flavor, cooking, yield, allergens, and storage.

What you need

  • 2 cups frozen mango chunks (~300 g)
  • 1 cup plain yogurt (~245 g)
  • 1 tsp vanilla (5 ml)
  • 1–2 Tbsp water, only if needed (15–30 ml)

Make it

  1. Blend the mango, yogurt, and vanilla until smooth, adding water only if the blender needs help.
  2. Divide among pop molds, add sticks, and freeze until firm.

The child can

Measure the yogurt and fill the molds with a small ladle.

The adult handles

Runs the blender, places the sticks, and manages the freezer.
Flavor & textureCool, thick, and creamy with a bright mango tang.
Allergens to checkMilk. Use a plain unsweetened dairy-free yogurt if that already fits the child's diet.
Age & textureLet pops soften 1–2 minutes so they are easy to bite. Adult decides the safe size for the child.

Learn together

Colour Watch: notice how the orange mango and white yogurt swirl into one shade. Guess the new colour before blending.

Try a swap: Swap mango for frozen peaches or pineapple.
Test still needed: Test sweetness, how easily the pops unmold, and the real number of pops.
